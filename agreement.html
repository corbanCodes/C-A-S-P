The inspection agreement

What you are signing, section by section, before you sign it rather than after. It runs to about four pages.

Why there is a contract at all

An inspection report gets used by people who were not in the room — a court, a lender, an insurer, a board, a buyer. The agreement is what makes it clear who the report was written for, what it covers, what it deliberately does not cover, and who carries which risk.

It also protects you. It fixes the price, states the insurance we carry, sets out when the deposit is refundable, and commits us to a scope you can hold us to.

Read section 7 in particular. Where an exterior elevated element poses an immediate threat to safety, we are required by statute to notify the local enforcement agency within 15 days. That is not a term we can negotiate away, and anybody offering to is telling you something useful about how they work.

What is in it

Twelve sections. None of them are surprising, which is rather the point.

1. Scope of work

Exactly which buildings, areas and elements are covered, which standard they are being measured against, and the date the inspection is scheduled for.

2. What is expressly excluded

A visual and dimensional inspection is not destructive testing. Concealed conditions, anything behind a finish and anything not reasonably accessible on the day are outside scope unless separately engaged.

3. Standard of care

The inspection is performed to the standard of a reasonably prudent certified inspector. A report is a professional opinion on observed conditions — it is not a warranty, a guarantee, or an insurance policy against future failure.

4. Insurance

The commercial general liability and professional liability (errors and omissions) cover carried by the firm, with limits stated, and certificates available on request.

5. Client obligations

Safe access to the property, keys and codes, a contact who can open locked areas, disclosure of known hazards, and any prior reports or permit history you already hold.

6. Limitation of liability & indemnity

The cap on liability, how claims are handled, and the indemnity each party gives the other. Read this section properly — it is the one your insurer will ask about.

7. Mandatory hazard reporting

You acknowledge that where an exterior elevated element poses an immediate threat to safety, we are required by statute to notify the local enforcement agency within 15 days. That obligation is not waivable and is not ours to negotiate.

8. Confidentiality & third-party reliance

The report is prepared for you. Who else may rely on it — a lender, a buyer, a board, opposing counsel — and on what terms.

9. Fees & payment terms

The quoted fee, the 20% deposit that confirms the booking, and the balance due on completion. The report is released when the balance clears.

10. Scheduling, cancellation & rescheduling

Notice periods, what happens if the site is not accessible on the day, and the circumstances in which the deposit is refundable.

11. Governing law & disputes

California law, the venue for any dispute, and the dispute resolution steps that come before anyone files anything.

12. Electronic signature consent

Your consent to sign and receive documents electronically under the federal ESIGN Act and the California Uniform Electronic Transactions Act.

Signed electronically

Sent to your email, signed on a phone in about two minutes. Valid under the federal ESIGN Act and California’s Uniform Electronic Transactions Act.

Nothing charged to sign

Signing costs nothing. The deposit link comes afterwards, and the booking is not held until it is paid.

Your copy, kept

A countersigned PDF lands in your inbox and stays in your client portal alongside the report.

This page is a plain-English summary. The agreement itself is the operative document, and where the two differ the agreement governs. Nothing here is legal advice — if the terms matter to you, and on a commercial engagement they should, have your own counsel read it.
