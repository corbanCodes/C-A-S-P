Who actually files these lawsuits (the numbers will annoy you)

It is not a wave of aggrieved customers. It is a small, professional, extremely productive group of plaintiffs and firms — and the state publishes the receipts.

Robert LehmanFounder, Inspector Group California · July 15, 2026 · 5 min read

When owners imagine an accessibility lawsuit, they imagine a wronged customer. The data describes something else: a compact industry. Knowing how it actually works is the difference between vague worry and a rational plan.

The concentration is the story

In 2025, plaintiffs filed 3,252 federal ADA Title III lawsuits in California — the most of any state, around 271 a month. In 2024, a single plaintiff organization filed 2,598 of that year's suits by itself. And of all the demand letters and complaints reported to the California Commission on Disability Access in 2024, 95.8% came from just ten law firms — one firm alone accounted for roughly 41%. These are not our estimates; attorneys are required by Civil Code §55.32 to send copies of demand letters to the Commission, which tabulates them quarterly. Sources here.

How the targeting works

Parking lots are legible from the street and, increasingly, from satellite and street-view imagery: stall counts, striping, van aisles, ramps and slopes photograph well. Entrances and counters take one short visit. A practiced filer does not need to find a sympathetic disaster; they need to find one measurable barrier, because statutory damages are set by statute — $4,000 minimum per occasion — and the attorney-fee award makes modest cases worth running at volume.

Two wrong conclusions to avoid

First: “these suits are frivolous.” Usually not, and that is the problem — the barrier named in the complaint is typically real and measurable. The industry is opportunistic; the violations are genuine. Judges know the difference, which is why the defense of “but everyone does it” goes nowhere.

Second: “I'm small, they won't bother.” The volume numbers say otherwise. Small retail, restaurants and strip-mall tenants are the core of the docket precisely because they are plentiful, visibly imperfect, and motivated to settle fast.

The rational response

You cannot control who drives past your parking lot. You can control which procedural category you are in when they do. A CASp inspection done before any claim exists is what California's own CRASCA statute offers as the escape: qualified-defendant status, the possibility of a 90-day stay and an early evaluation conference, reduced damages tiers, and — more practically — a property that stops being an easy pick, because the easy findings got fixed on your schedule instead of theirs.

This is orientation, not legal advice. Statutes are summarized and cited so you can check them; how they apply to your property depends on facts we have not seen. For an answer about your building, book the inspection or start with a call — and for anything in active litigation, retain California counsel.
